Caring for Automatic Timepieces: Cleaning and Servicing Guidelines

Atelier guidelines on watch winder settings, moisture prevention, gaskets, and quartz comparisons.

Swiss Escapement Geometry: The Core Regulator

The timing accuracy of a mechanical watch depends entirely on its escapement structure. The lever escapement, consisting of an escape wheel, a pallet fork, and ruby pallet jewels, translates the rotational force of the mainspring into precise timing oscillations. The pallet fork acts as a mechanical gate, locking and releasing the gear train at exact intervals. This mechanical interaction is what creates the characteristic ticking sound of an automatic watch.

Calibrating the escapement requires adjusting the angles of the ruby pallets. If the pallet fork releases the escape wheel too early, the gear train will spin out, causing the watch to run too fast. If it locks too tightly, the friction will stall the balance wheel, stopping the movement completely. Our master calibrators adjust these components under high-magnification microscopes, ensuring the balance wheel swings at its target amplitude of 280 degrees for optimal accuracy.

Balance Wheel Tuning and Hairspring Regulation

The balance wheel and hairspring form the regulating organ of the watch movement, functioning similarly to a pendulum in a clock. The hairspring, a micro-thin coiled metal band, must compress and expand with absolute symmetry. If the spring bends or touches an adjacent coil, it will distort the timing rate. Horologists adjust the active length of the hairspring using regulation screws or sliding levers on the balance bridge.

Additionally, temperature changes can cause hairspring metals to expand or contract, affecting timing. We solve this by using modern paramagnetic alloys that remain stable across extreme temperature variations. We also adjust the weight balance of the balance wheel. By turning micro-screws along the wheel rim, watchmakers balance its weight distribution, ensuring it oscillates symmetrically in all positions, whether resting flat or held vertical.

Synthetic Jewels and Synthetic Lubrication

A watch movement contains dozens of turning pivots that generate friction under load. Without proper jewel bearings, steel pivots would wear down copper plates within months. We use synthetic corundum rubies as bearing jewels. Rubies are extremely hard, second only to diamond, and provide a glassy surface for steel gear pivots. This reduction in friction is vital for maintaining timing amplitude.

Lubricating these ruby bearings requires absolute precision. Watchmakers apply microscopic drops of synthetic oils, using custom micro-pipette tips under magnification. The oil must sit within the jewel sink without leaking onto the gears, as misplaced oil attracts dust that turns into abrasive paste. Applying different oil viscosity ratings to high-speed wheels and high-torque mainspring gears guarantees smooth operation for decades.

Mainspring Energy Management and Power Reserve

The mainspring, housed within the mainspring barrel, serves as the energy storage bank for the entire watch. As you turn the crown or swing your arm, the spring winds tightly, storing mechanical energy. This energy is released through the mainspring barrel teeth to the center wheel, driving the timing hands. Managing the torque output of the mainspring is a key challenge in horological engineering.

As the mainspring unwinds, its torque output decreases, which can cause the watch to run slower. We address this torque drop by utilizing custom spring shapes and maintaining stable barrel friction. Some high-end movements feature dual barrels to extend the power reserve up to 72 hours, ensuring the watch maintains constant force and accurate timing even when off the wrist for a weekend.

Titanium Casing Architecture and Water Proofing

Protecting the watch movement from moisture, dust, and magnetic fields requires a strong casing. We mill our watch cases from solid blocks of grade-5 titanium, an alloy valued for its strength-to-weight ratio and resistance to corrosion. The case features screw-down crowns, threaded case backs, and thick rubber gaskets that seal all potential entry points.

Before a watch is certified, we test its case inside high-pressure chambers to verify its depth rating. Diver watches require helium escape valves to prevent the sapphire glass from popping off during decompression. We also polish the case surfaces using custom diamond wheels, creating a contrast between brushed slate grains and mirror-polished chamfers that highlights the case architecture.

Caring for Mechanical Timepieces: Maintenance Rituals

A luxury mechanical watch requires periodic maintenance to preserve its timing accuracy and water resistance. We recommend sending your watch to our Mercer workshop every five years for servicing. During a full service, our technicians disassemble the movement, clean all components in ultrasonic baths, inspect for pivot wear, and rebuild with fresh synthetic lubrication.

Additionally, we recommend checking the gasket seals every two years if you swim with your watch regularly. Avoid exposing your mechanical watch to strong magnetic fields, such as speakers or laptops, as magnetized hairsprings will stick together and run fast. If your watch gets magnetized, our concierge can quickly demagnetize it using custom coil tools.

Historical Chronometers and Navigation

In addition to these core mechanical sections, we encourage watch enthusiasts to look at historical pocket watches and clock towers. The transition from large gravity-powered pendulum weights to micro-thin mainsprings and balance wheels is one of the most interesting stories in human technology. Early navigators used custom chronometers to calculate longitude across oceans, saving thousands of lives and mapping the globe. Today, wearing an automatic watch is a connection to this historical journey of scientific discovery.

A hand-calibrated mechanical watch is a functional work of art.
